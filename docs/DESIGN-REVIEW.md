# MASSFRONT — Design Review

*Written against the current build (app_version 1.12.0, `design/massfront-design.db`), cross-checked against `src/game/ai.js`, `src/game/sim.js`, `src/game/economy.js`, `src/develop.js`, `src/endgame.js`, `src/daily.js`, `src/factions.js` and `src/tutorial.js`. A prior pass (`docs/REVIEW.md`) covered rendering and low-level correctness in detail and is not repeated here except where it changes what a player experiences. A lot of what that review flagged — the rubber-band AI, the free Bulwark shield, the missing move order, the settings-screen onboarding — has visibly been fixed since it was written. This review is about what's true today.*

---

## 1. The core loop

A match is: drop the carrier, root an economy (Extractor, Reactor, Factory), pick a unit mix, and hold or push against a clock that gets steadily less forgiving. That skeleton is sound and it is the correct skeleton — it's Supreme Commander's, scaled to a phone.

The moment-to-moment texture is better than it used to be. There is now a real move order separate from attack-move (`moveMode` in `src/ui/input.js`), formations (spread/line/wedge/box) that face the direction of travel, hold-position, patrol, and control groups that store a generation tag so a dead unit can't be silently replaced by a stranger in the same slot. Combat modes are genuine trades now, not free upgrades: Guard is −45% damage taken but also −55% damage *dealt*, Overdrive redlines for 14 seconds before it auto-cuts rather than the 48 seconds a unit will rarely live to see, and Siege takes 35% more damage while rooted. That's four or five real decisions a player makes per engagement, which is a defensible amount for a touchscreen RTS.

Where it sags: once the opening build order is down (Extractor → Reactor → Factory, repeat), the mid-game is largely reactive. The AI's harassment squads retarget your weakest Extractor on a fixed clock (65–110s depending on difficulty) and its waves arrive on a compressing timer (starts at 58–104s depending on difficulty, floors at 30–62s by wave 15). Between those two clocks there isn't much for the player to *decide* — mostly queue more of whatever's cost-efficient and wait for the next ping. The commander abilities (orbital blast, lance, NOVA strike, and the researchable EMP) are the main lever available during that dead time, and only one of the four is available without spending real progression (Orbital Uplink is 1200 cores; EMP requires the full Xenology research branch). A new-ish player spends the middle third of most matches with comparatively little to press.

The good news is that the fix is cheap relative to the systems already built: the harassment and wave clocks already exist and are already tuned to create pressure at a legible cadence — the game just doesn't give the player enough to *do* between pings. Widening ability access, or adding a small number of match-scoped choices (a tech pick, a supply-drop choice, anything that fires on a similar cadence to the AI's clocks) would fill that gap without new simulation work.

## 2. Economy and balance

Computing damage-per-cost and HP-per-cost across the 25 costed combat/support units (cost modeled as `mass + energy/4`, matching the game's own ~1:4 mass:energy exchange), a few things stand out that a player will find within their first few matches:

**Striker is a clear outlier.** At 8 mass / 34 energy (cost ≈16.5) it returns 0.653 DPS per unit of cost — more than *any other unit in the game*, including the next-best ground combat unit, Pyro, at 0.362 (cost 80). Its HP-per-cost (3.33) is also near the top of the whole roster. Five Strikers cost the same as one Pyro (80 vs 80) and deliver 54 raw DPS and 275 HP against Pyro's 28.9 DPS and 240 HP — nearly double the damage for more health, split across five bodies instead of one. This is not automatically "broken" — Pyro and other splash weapons (Cinder aoe 46, Scorcher aoe 38, Reaper aoe 52) exist specifically to punish five bodies standing together, and Striker's tiny 55 HP means it dies to almost anything in one or two hits — but it means the correct opening move in nearly every game is "spam the cheapest unit," and the interesting play only starts once the opponent commits to countering that with splash. Right now that counter isn't taught anywhere the player would see it before losing an army to it once.

**Pyro/Scorcher and Thumper/Bombard are the same shape of pair, twice.** Both pairs share a chassis (Scorcher literally reuses the `pyro` sprite) and a role, and in both cases the more expensive unit is a straight scale-up: Scorcher costs 2.4× Pyro for 1.5× the DPS and 2.7× the HP; Bombard costs 2.5× Thumper for *identical* DPS (13.6 vs 13.57 — the numbers only differ by rounding) and 3× the HP at 1.5× the range. Neither expensive unit is strictly dominated — more HP and range are real, especially against Skyguard/turret fire — but neither is a different *tactical idea* from its cheaper sibling either. The dps-per-cost gap (Thumper 0.120 vs Bombard 0.048, a 2.5× swing) means the efficient army always wants the cheap version and only reaches for the expensive one when raw durability matters more than the trade. That's a legitimate late-game turtle-vs-tempo choice, but it reads to the player as "the same unit, but I have more cores now," not as a new tool.

**Bulwark has exactly one counter in the entire 31-unit roster.** The shield is metered now (5 energy/tick upkeep, per `upkeepE` in the unit table) and Sonic weapons pierce it (`WK_PIERCE={s:1}` in `sim.js`) — both real fixes to what the prior review correctly flagged as an unanswerable defensive unit. But Resonator is the *only* unit in the game with a Sonic weapon (`wk:'s'`). "The counter to the best defensive unit in the game is one specific mid-tier vehicle" is a thin answer dressed up as a real one; if a player hasn't researched or noticed Resonator, Bulwark is still effectively unanswerable to them.

**Naval is dead content on three of four maps.** Corvette and Dreadnought (and the Harbor that builds them) require a shoreline, and of the four maps in the `maps` table only Shattered Isles (`hBias:-0.062`, described as "Drowned world — navies rule") is built to have enough water to make them relevant; Vanguard Valley, Highland Scar and Relic Basin are described as plains, cliffs and a crater respectively. That's a full production building and two units — 15% of the combat roster — that see meaningful play on one map in four.

## 3. The opponent

`ai.js` has clearly been reworked since the engineering review was written, and the rework is good design, not just a bug fix. The headline change: `aiThreat()` now scales off **match time only**, not the player's unit count, structure count or commander level (the old version reportedly drove Hard's income multiplier to 9.6× off the player's own success, which is a punishment dressed as difficulty). The new curve is a flat, learnable clock capped at 1.35×/1.7×/2.2× income for Easy/Normal/Hard. On top of it sits an explicit **Threat Level** the player opts into (see §4) — so difficulty the player didn't ask for is gone, and difficulty they did ask for is allowed to be steep.

The wave logic has real tactical shape now. Waves won't launch under-strength (`army<need` holds the timer and re-checks, up to a cap so a boxed-in AI doesn't stall forever); target selection is value-weighted rather than pure proximity, so an undefended forty-mass Reactor can't decoy every wave for the rest of the match; and there's a flanking check — if the direct line to the target is defended, the wave swings to the weaker of two flanking points. There's also composition-reactive building: more than two player aircraft spawns Skyguards, and a hive infestation past tier 2 spawns Hellstorms. Retreats are gated on wave size (a four-unit wave can't "rout" on its first casualty) and tracked by slot *and* generation, so a dead unit's replacement can't be silently counted as a survivor.

That's a legitimately well-built pressure generator: predictable enough to learn, varied enough (three factions' worth of build bias, described in §5) to not feel identical every match, and honest about what it's doing (the threat clock is visible in the Operations screen, not hidden).

It is not, however, an opponent that plays *against you* in the sense of reading your army and adapting its composition. The reactive building is narrow — air and swarm-HP triggers exist, but there's no equivalent for "the player just fielded fifteen Bulwarks, build Resonators," or "the player is turtling behind Sentinels, mass artillery instead of frontal armor." The AI's intelligence is almost entirely in *when* and *where* to attack, not *what* to attack with beyond its faction's fixed bias table. For a first release that's a fine tradeoff — reading composition well is a hard problem — but it's worth naming precisely: this is a wave-based pressure system with good pacing and target selection, not a strategic opponent.

## 4. Progression

This is where the game currently asks the most of a solo developer's maintenance budget, and where I'd point first if asked what to simplify.

There are five progression surfaces live at once: **Armory** (`storePerks`, 11 entries, permanent percentage buffs bought with cores — HP, damage, income, structure HP, ability cooldown), **Research/Development** (`develop.js`, a 12-node tree gating module crafting, paid in cores *and* one of four raw materials, where modules grant similar percentage buffs but wear out over 5–14 matches and have to be re-crafted), **Daily Orders** (10 rotating objectives, deterministic per calendar day, paying cores and timed boosters), **Account Rank** (a 10-tier XP ladder, `RANKS` in `meta.js`) and the **Endgame layer** added specifically to answer "why play match fifty" — a Threat ladder (12 permanent levels), priced Operation Modifiers, a weekly fixed contract, and a 12-box faction-×-map Mastery grid.

Individually each is reasoned-through — the comments in `endgame.js` explicitly name the four questions the layer is meant to answer, and that's genuinely good design thinking. Together they share currencies in a way that isn't explained anywhere: cores fund *both* the Armory and Research, and Armory buffs are strictly less effort than the equivalent Research buff (permanent vs. wears out in materials-plus-cores every several matches) for a similar percentage outcome, so a purely rational player has real reason to ignore Research entirely except for the module effects Armory can't grant (the combined HP+damage Relic Lattice, the EMP ability). That's a genuine strategic choice buried inside what looks like redundancy, and nothing in the UI says so.

Account Rank is worth flagging on its own: I checked whether it gates anything (`grep`'d for rank-based unlocks across `meta.js` and the rest of `src/`) and found none — `metaRankIdx()` is read only for display. It is a second, permanent "level" sitting alongside the in-match Commander level (`heroLvl`, which *does* gate build-menu unlocks during a match and resets every game). Two visually similar level systems, one of which does nothing mechanically, is exactly the kind of thing a new player reasonably confuses and a solo dev has to maintain for no return.

**The one outright bug in this section**, and the most important finding in this review: `endgame.js`'s Operation Modifiers and the older `WILDCARDS` array in `meta.js` are two separate ID lists that were only partially reconciled. `pickWildcards()` bridges them by looking up each chosen Operation Modifier's ID in the `WILDCARDS` table and only applying it if found. Five of the ten Operation Modifiers — **Hiveworld** (`swarm`), **Blitz Doctrine** (`blitz`), **Brittle Frames** (`brittle`), **No Salvage** (`nofab`) and **Blood Moon** (`dark`, which collides with the *different* ID `moon` that the old Blood Moon wildcard actually uses) — have no matching entry in `WILDCARDS` at all. I confirmed by grepping every `WC.<id>` read across `sim.js`, `ai.js`, `economy.js` and `hud.js`: none of those five tokens are ever consumed by gameplay. Selecting all five in the Operations screen adds their full reward weight — 0.35+0.35+0.40+0.20+0.10, a **+140% payout multiplier** (`opModMult()`, feeding directly into `matchScore()` and the Weekly leaderboard) — to a match that is, mechanically, completely unmodified. A player who finds this (and someone will) can post an inflated Weekly score for free, which undermines the one system in the endgame layer whose entire point is that scores are comparable.

## 5. Faction identity

There are four factions in the data, but only one is playable: Nova Federation is explicitly the player's own banner and — per the comment in `factions.js` — "never appears in the enemy picker." The other three (Red Ascendancy / Legion, Syndicate Coalition, Umbral Brood / Horde) are the selectable AI opponents, and they draw from the same 31-unit roster the player uses, differentiated by three things: an economy multiplier (`income`/`waveMul`/`buildMul` — Horde is notably poorer on paper at 0.92 income but compensates with 1.25× build speed and a much higher wave send-fraction), a build-weight bias table, and a visual kit (scale/squash/tilt/hull-and-glow-colour, described in the source comments as deliberately more than a recolour — Legion is heavier and slower-looking, Syndicate is hover-based with no treads, Horde reads as grown carapace rather than machined hull).

The build bias is thoughtfully chosen and it works: Legion's weights land on Goliath, Thumper, Bombard and Harbinger — armor and siege, matching its "heavy armor columns" description. Syndicate's land on Wasp, Raptor, Kestrel and Resonator — three of four are aircraft, matching "air superiority and lightning raids." Horde's land on Striker, Pyro and Cinder — the cheapest infantry and splash units in the game, matching "endless cheap swarms." Fighting each faction does feel different in practice, because you're facing a genuinely different composition each time. This part earns its keep.

What doesn't earn its keep is the Codex. `factionLore` (and the `FACART` table it mirrors in `factions.js`) gives all four factions — including Nova — a four-unit named roster: Ascendancy fields "WARDEN, BLOODHOUND, RAVAGER, OVERSEER"; Syndicate fields "RAIDER, PHANTOM, VULTURE, DECRYPTOR"; Horde fields "SPAWNLING, CARVER, BLOATER, MAW"; Nova fields "STRIKER, GUARDIAN, SKYHAWK, SENTINEL." None of these names correspond to the actual unit roster except by loose analogy (Striker is real; "Guardian," "Skyhawk," "Sentinel," and all eight of the AI factions' names are not — the real roster is Rhino, Wasp, Warden, Vulture, and so on, and "Vulture" is a real *Syndicate-biased* unit while the Codex assigns the name "VULTURE" to Syndicate as a "Drone Gunship," which happens to roughly match, but the other eleven names don't). A curious new player who reads the faction Codex before their first match — which is exactly the kind of player this screen is built for — will go looking for a Bloodhound or a Decryptor and never find one. This isn't a balance problem, it's unreconciled content: the lore pass and the roster implementation were written at different times and never checked against each other. It's cheap to fix (rename four roster units per faction to match the Codex, or rewrite sixteen lines of Codex copy to match the roster) and it's currently costing the game its best "faction identity" asset for free.

## 6. The first ten minutes

This is a genuinely different experience than the one the earlier review described, and it's worth saying plainly: onboarding was rebuilt, and it's now good. `tutorial.js` implements KEEL, a first-drop tutorial that tests real game state (`hasPlayerBld('mex')`, not a scripted step counter) rather than a fixed sequence, so a player who builds out of the expected order doesn't get stuck waiting on a step they've already completed. It's voiced in-fiction rather than as a settings dialog, and it's skippable and re-enterable from Settings. Wildcards default off (`wcChoice` reads `META.wcPref|0`, which is `0` for a player with no saved preference) — a new player's first match is not rolled with early enemy titans or −40% vision, which was a real problem in the earlier build and clearly got fixed.

Where a first-time player can still get lost: the first enemy wave lands on a clock (58–104s scaled by difficulty and faction) that starts as soon as `aiSetup()` runs, which is a tight window against a five-step tutorial (deploy → extractor → reactor → factory → queue an army) if the player is reading each KEEL line rather than skimming it. I did not verify whether the tutorial forces Easy difficulty or extends the first wave timer independently of the difficulty setting — if it doesn't, a slow-reading new player can plausibly take their first hit mid-tutorial, which is a bad first impression regardless of how good the tutorial copy is. Worth a five-minute check.

The deeper issue is the one described in §4 and §5: even a player who completes the tutorial and plays a dozen matches is looking at an Armory screen and a Develop screen that both sell overlapping percentage buffs with two different currencies and no explanation of why both exist, and a faction Codex that quietly lied to them about what they'd be fighting. Neither of those is a first-ten-minutes problem exactly, but they're both first-*week* problems, and they're the reason a new player's confusion doesn't fully resolve just because the tutorial is good.

## 7. What to cut

This is the section that matters most for a solo developer, because the honest answer is: nothing here needs to be deleted, but three things need to be merged or cut down before a fourth thing gets built on top of them.

**Merge Armory and Research into one buff surface, or make their difference load-bearing.** Right now they're two screens, two currencies (cores alone vs. cores-plus-materials), and overlapping outcomes (both grant HP%/damage%/energy%). The wear mechanic in Research is a good idea — "your power has a running cost" is a real design point the code's own comments make well — but it's undercut by Armory sitting right next to it offering the same categories of buff with no running cost at all. Either fold Armory's percentage tiers into the Research tree as permanent unlocks (so cores buy *access* and materials buy the *modules that expire*, one funnel instead of two), or cut Armory down to the two or three perks that don't overlap with a module (Supply Cache's flat match-start bonus, the Orbital unlock) and let Research own every recurring percentage. Either direction removes an entire screen's worth of future balance passes.

**Fix or delete the five dead Operation Modifiers.** This is the actual bug (§4) and it's small to fix: either add the five missing bridges to `WILDCARDS` so `pickWildcards()` can find them, or — faster, and arguably more honest given they were presumably meant to be simulation effects that never got wired up — drop them from `OPMODS` until they're implemented, so the Operations screen isn't selling reward multipliers for nothing.

**Decide what Account Rank is for, or cut it.** It's a second XP ladder next to the in-match Commander level and the Threat ladder, and right now it unlocks nothing. Either give it teeth (cosmetic commander colours, an Armory discount, an Operations Threat-level head start — something) or remove the screen and fold its XP into the number that already means something to the player, which is Threat.

**Naval is the one place I'd genuinely consider cutting content rather than merging systems.** Two units and a building that are live on one map in four is a maintenance line (balance, art, AI build logic) that returns very little. The cheaper fix is design, not deletion: give Harbor a land-adjacent function (even a resource bonus) so it's not simply absent from three-quarters of matches — but if that's not worth the time, cutting Corvette/Dreadnought down to Isles-only content (already effectively true) and being explicit about it in the map description is a legitimate, cheaper answer than pretending they're roster-wide.

Everything else earns its place. The endgame layer (Threat/Operations/Weekly/Mastery) is the right shape for "why play match fifty" and shouldn't be touched structurally — just have it stop paying out for nothing. The AI's wave/threat/target logic is good and should be left alone. The tutorial is good and should be left alone. Combat modes, control groups, formations and move-order are all correctly built genre-standard RTS controls and are not maintenance risk — they're finished.

## Next five things, ranked

1. **Fix the dead Operation Modifiers.** (Small — a few hours.) This is a live exploit against the game's own leaderboard system. Either wire the five missing IDs into `WC` or pull them from `OPMODS` until they are. Ship this before anything else in this document.

2. **Teach the splash counter to massed cheap infantry.** (Small–medium — a day or two.) Striker's cost efficiency isn't a balance bug, it's a fine emergent strategy that the game never tells the player how to beat. A single coach line the first time a player loses a Striker-heavy push to an AoE unit, or a card-level note ("AoE weapons punish grouped light units"), would convert the sharpest balance edge in the roster into a taught mechanic instead of a silent one.

3. **Reconcile the faction Codex with the actual roster.** (Small–medium — one focused pass, mostly writing.) Sixteen unit-name/description lines across four factions, checked against the 31 real units and either renamed to match or rewritten to describe what's actually fielded. This is the cheapest way to make "four factions" feel true rather than aspirational, and it's pure content work with no simulation risk.

4. **Merge Armory into Research, or cut Armory to its non-overlapping perks.** (Medium — a few days, mostly UI and save-data migration.) This is the highest-leverage simplification in the game for a solo maintainer: one fewer screen, one fewer currency relationship to explain, and it removes the "why do both of these exist" question a player currently has to shrug past.

5. **Give the mid-match player more to press between the AI's clocks.** (Medium-large — the harassment/wave cadence is already tuned, so this is content, not systems work.) Widen ability access below the Orbital Uplink's 1200-core price, or add one small match-scoped choice on a similar cadence to the wave timer. The core loop's floor is already good; this is what raises its ceiling.
